If you have applied for a cash credit or overdraft limit, your bank has probably asked for "CMA data". It sounds technical. It is really a structured way of showing a lender how your business uses money, and how much of that money the lender should provide.

## What it stands for

CMA stands for Credit Monitoring Arrangement. The data is a set of standard statements that banks use to assess and monitor working capital and term lending.

## What it contains

A typical CMA report includes:

- **Past and projected operating statements:** sales, costs and profit, usually for the last two or three years and the next one or more.
- **Balance sheet analysis:** assets and liabilities arranged the way a credit officer reads them.
- **Current assets and current liabilities:** stock, debtors, creditors and other short-term items, with the holding period for each.
- **Working capital assessment:** how the lender arrives at the limit it is willing to give.
- **Ratios and fund flow:** liquidity, leverage and how funds have moved over the period.

## Why lenders rely on it

Your financial statements show what happened. CMA data shows what the lender is being asked to fund and whether the numbers hang together: whether projected sales are consistent with your stock and debtor levels, whether margins look realistic, and whether the business can carry the limit it is asking for.

## Where it goes wrong

- Projections that jump without an explanation the lender can check.
- Holding levels that don't match the stock and debtor statements you submit.
- Figures that don't reconcile with audited financials or GST returns.

Any of these leads to queries, a smaller limit, or a decline.

## What to do

Treat CMA data as an argument, not a form. Every projection should be traceable to something real: an order book, a capacity increase, a pricing change. When it is, the conversation with the credit officer becomes about how much, not whether.
